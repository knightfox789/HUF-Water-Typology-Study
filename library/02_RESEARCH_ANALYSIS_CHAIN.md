# Research Analysis Chain — Water Typology Study

## Purpose
Reverse-engineer the research report into a transparent analytical chain that can later be converted into a matrix, calculation engine, dashboard or updated HUF methodology.

## Master chain

**Rainfall + Geology/Aquifer + Soil**
→ establish natural/static water setting
→ define fundamental typologies
→ add Surface Water + Groundwater + Irrigation Infrastructure
→ estimate Water Supply
→ analyse Agriculture + Livestock + Domestic + Industrial use
→ estimate Water Demand
→ analyse Water & Soil Quality
→ compare Supply vs Demand
→ validate selected variables using primary watershed data
→ normalize/classify dynamic parameters
→ derive water-security scenarios
→ identify hotspots
→ define region-specific interventions.

---

## 1. Rainfall

**Historical rainfall data**
→ spatial distribution
→ long-term average
→ temporal/five-year trend
→ regional comparison
→ relationship with groundwater level/recharge
→ water-availability interpretation.

Variables include annual/monthly rainfall, regional averages, trend and spatial variation.

Key interpretive result: rainfall trend alone does not explain groundwater trend. In some regions rainfall rises while groundwater continues to decline, indicating the importance of extraction, aquifer behaviour, cropping and recharge pathways.

## 2. Surface Water

**Rainfall/runoff + river basins + reservoirs + local storage**
→ surface-water availability
→ district/regional comparison
→ irrigated/rainfed pattern
→ contribution to total water supply.

Inputs include major, medium and minor irrigation schemes, SSNNL systems, rivers/basins, check dams, ponds, farm ponds and related GIS layers.

## 3. Water Harvesting Structures

**Structure inventory**
→ structure type

This makes governance part of infrastructure performance.

## 4. Irrigation Source

**Canal + tank + tubewell + open well + other source**
→ temporal source-wise irrigated area
→ source transition
→ regional dependency
→ groundwater risk.

Study-level pattern:

**Open/shallow wells decline**
→ **tube/deep wells increase**
while
**canal irrigation also expands**.

Therefore, surface-water development does not automatically replace groundwater abstraction.

Key interpretation:

**Canal expansion + groundwater pumping may grow simultaneously**

## 6. Groundwater — Aquifer Framework

**Geology**
→ aquifer type
→ storage/yield/recharge behaviour
→ groundwater level
→ availability
→ abstraction response
→ groundwater quality
→ sustainability.

Principal hydrogeological settings include alluvial, volcanic/basalt, soft sedimentary and crystalline/hard-rock systems.

Important logic:
- Hard-rock aquifers can show rapid water-level response to pumping.
- Large alluvial aquifers may retain large storage but can still experience long-term depletion and water-quality deterioration.

## 7. Groundwater Level

**Observation well data**
→ pre-monsoon level
→ post-monsoon level
→ seasonal fluctuation
→ decadal/long-term trend
→ regional comparison
→ rainfall relationship
→ groundwater-stress interpretation.

Groundwater level must be read jointly with aquifer type, extraction and irrigation intensity.

---

## 8. Groundwater Availability

**Recharge/resources dataset**
→ available groundwater volume
→ district/regional comparison
→ normalized availability where required
→ relationship with demand and stage of development.

Important distinction:

**High groundwater availability ≠ groundwater security.**

A district can have a large aquifer resource but also severe extraction pressure.

---

## 9. Groundwater Quality

**Water quality samples/data**
→ EC/TDS
→ nitrate
→ fluoride
→ iron
→ arsenic
→ permissible-limit comparison
→ hotspot mapping
→ relationship with geology, agriculture and pumping.

Likely analytical pathways emphasized in the report:

**Agricultural intensification + fertilizer use**
→ nitrate risk.

**Over-extraction/deeper abstraction**
→ salinity/fluoride and other quality concerns.

Thus quantity and quality cannot be treated separately.

---

## 10. Primary vs Secondary Groundwater Data

**Secondary government dataset**
→ field measurement in representative watershed
→ compare GWL / EC / TDS
→ validate or identify divergence
→ assess suitability of district-scale data.

Key lesson:

**District averages can conceal local aquifer and water-quality conditions.**

Future work should favour finer spatial resolution where management decisions are local.

---

## 11. Soil Type

**Geology + climate + soil formation**
→ texture/type
→ infiltration
→ water-holding capacity
→ drainage
→ crop suitability
→ irrigation requirement
→ waterlogging/salinity risk.

Typical logic:
- Sandy soil → lower retention → greater irrigation frequency/requirement.
- Deep black/clayey soil → high retention → excess irrigation can increase waterlogging risk.
- Sandy saline/coastal soils → salinity and water-quality interactions.

## 13. Waterlogging, Salinity and Land Degradation

**Canal command + high irrigation + flat/clayey terrain + weak drainage**
→ waterlogging
→ salt accumulation
→ soil degradation
→ crop productivity impact
→ possible groundwater-quality impact.

This is an important example of a supply-side intervention producing secondary environmental risks when drainage and demand management are weak.

## 14. Agriculture

**Season**
→ crop
→ crop area
→ yield
→ sales price
→ irrigation source
→ crop-water requirement
→ actual water use
→ return
→ water-use/economic classification.

Three seasonal systems plus horticulture are analysed.

Agriculture is the dominant demand-side driver in the state water balance.

---

## 15. Cropping-Area Trend

**Expansion of Rabi/Summer/Horticulture area**
→ higher cropping intensity
→ more irrigation events
→ greater annual abstraction
→ higher agricultural demand.

Thus water-resource development can enable agricultural expansion that offsets expected water savings.

---

## 16. Micro-Irrigation

**Drip/sprinkler adoption**
→ lower water application per irrigation/hectare in theory
→ potential field efficiency improvement.

But:

**Water saving per hectare**
→ expansion of irrigated area / additional crop / shift to higher-value crops
→ total abstraction may remain unchanged or increase.

Hence:

**Micro-irrigation efficiency ≠ basin/aquifer water saving unless total use is controlled.**

---

## 17. Actual Crop Water Utilization

Field-method chain:

**Pump HP + water head + pump/motor efficiency**
→ calculate pump discharge
→ discharge × pumping hours
→ water per irrigation
→ × number of irrigation/pumping days
→ total crop-period water
→ convert to cubic metres
→ divide by crop area
→ actual water use per hectare
→ crop-wise mean.

Then:

**Actual field water use**
versus
**SIP crop-water requirement**
→ excess/deficit percentage.

---

## 18. Actual Water Use vs SIP Requirement

**Measured irrigation use**
→ compare recommended requirement
→ calculate percentage difference
→ identify inefficient/high-use crops and regions
→ revise agricultural demand.

Key report message: actual farmer water application is materially higher than theoretical/recommended values for many crops and regions.

---

## 19. Crop Economics × Water Demand

**Sales price/return**
+
**water demand**
→ normalize values
→ classify High/Low
→ four quadrants.

### Q1 — High Return / High Water Demand
Maintain economic value but reduce water requirement through varieties, agronomy and irrigation efficiency.

### Q2 — High Return / Low Water Demand
Preferred crops for promotion.

### Q3 — Low Return / High Water Demand
Priority for crop diversification and/or major efficiency/market intervention.

### Q4 — Low Return / Low Water Demand
Water-security friendly but requires value addition and market improvement.

**Rainfall**
→ runoff/recharge
→ **Surface Water + Groundwater**

# Central interpretation

The report's analytical logic supports the following principles:

- More rainfall does not automatically mean groundwater recovery.
- More structures do not automatically mean water security.
- More canal water does not automatically reduce groundwater pumping.
- More micro-irrigation does not automatically reduce total abstraction.
- Agricultural growth can increase water stress through higher cropping intensity.
- Dairy has both direct and indirect water-demand effects.
- Groundwater quantity, groundwater quality, soil quality and agriculture must be analysed together.
- Local institutions are necessary for durable infrastructure and resource governance.

Therefore:

**Water Security = Supply + Demand + Quality + Aquifer + Agriculture + Institutions.**
